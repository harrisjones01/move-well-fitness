HIIT vs Steady Cardio: What the Research Says

Both work. They do different jobs, and most people pick the wrong one.

MF
Marcus Feld 1 October 2026 · 8 min read

HIIT is marketed as the efficient option, and that is partly true: interval training improves VO2 max and cardiovascular fitness at least as much as steady-state work in a fraction of the time. What the marketing leaves out is the recovery cost, the injury rate in beginners, and the fact that most people do not need maximum efficiency — they need something they can repeat for years.

What each one is actually good at

HIIT

  • Improving VO2 max and cardiac output quickly.
  • Time efficiency — 15–20 minutes is a real session.
  • Improving insulin sensitivity.
  • Downside: high recovery cost, higher injury risk if technique is poor, and unpleasant enough that adherence is lower.

Steady cardio (zone 2)

  • Building aerobic base: more mitochondria, better fat oxidation, stronger heart.
  • Very low recovery cost — you can do it daily, alongside strength training.
  • Highly sustainable and easy to fit into a life (walking, cycling, commuting).
  • Downside: time. It asks for 30–60 minutes to be useful, and it is boring.

The split that works for most people

For general health and fitness, a week that looks roughly like this outperforms any extreme:

  1. 2–3 strength sessions (full body, 25–40 minutes).
  2. 2–3 easy zone-2 sessions (30–45 minutes of walking, cycling or rowing).
  3. 1 HIIT session (15–20 minutes), optional and never on a strength day.
  4. Movement snacks the rest of the time: walking, stairs, standing breaks.

The 80/20 idea

Endurance athletes have long used a rough 80/20 split — 80% of training easy, 20% hard. It applies surprisingly well to general fitness: most of your cardio should be genuinely easy, with a small amount of genuinely hard work.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Turning easy cardio into moderate cardio: a pace that is too hard to be recovery and too easy to be a real interval. You end up tired, recovering poorly, and getting less adaptation than if you had gone slower for longer or harder for shorter. If you cannot hold a conversation, you are not doing zone 2. If you could do it for an hour, you are not doing HIIT.
