Your First Week at the Gym: A Survival Guide

What to bring, what the machines are for, and the etiquette nobody explains.

NH
Dr. Nadia Haddad 22 October 2026 · 8 min read

Nobody in a gym is looking at you. This is not a platitude — it is an observation from about ten thousand hours of gym floors: people are looking at the clock, their own set, their phone, or the mirror. The person you are worried about noticing you is worried about being noticed too.

What follows is the practical stuff that nobody explains, which is the actual barrier for most first-timers.

What to bring

  • A water bottle and a small towel — the towel is etiquette, not vanity.
  • Flat, stable shoes. Running shoes with thick soft soles are genuinely bad for squatting and deadlifting.
  • Clothes you can move in. No dress code beyond covering the basics.
  • A phone with your session written down. Deciding what to do between sets is how a forty-minute session becomes ninety.
  • Optional: a padlock if the gym has lockers, and headphones if you prefer your own music.

Where to actually start

Skip the machines for the first session and spend twenty minutes on the cardio equipment at an easy pace. Not for fitness — for familiarity. You are learning where things are, how the entry gates work, and what the room feels like. Then do three simple movements: a goblet squat, a push-up or chest press, and a row.

That is a complete first session. Nobody's first workout should be a five-exercise, four-set commitment — the goal of session one is to make session two likely.

The machines are easier than they look

Most machines have a diagram showing the movement and the muscles, an adjustable seat with a pin, and a weight stack with a pin. Adjust the seat so the joint you are working lines up with the machine's pivot — if you are not sure, the diagram will show it. Start at the lightest plate and do one set there. Nobody is timing you.

The etiquette nobody explains

  1. Put your weights back. This is the only rule that everyone notices and nobody forgives.
  2. Do not stand in front of the dumbbell rack to do your set — step back so others can reach.
  3. Wipe down benches and machines after you use them. Towels exist for this.
  4. If someone is mid-set, wait. Do not start a conversation, and do not walk between them and the mirror.
  5. Sharing is normal: if someone asks how many sets you have left, it is a request to work in, not a complaint. Say 'two, you can jump in' if you are happy to share.
  6. Re-rack your plates. Every gym has a rule about this, and it is enforced entirely socially.
  7. Do not drop weights unless the gym has a platform designed for it. And do not slam the stack on the cable machine — it is loud and it damages equipment.

The question everyone is too shy to ask

Yes, you can ask for help. Staffed gyms have people paid to show you how a machine works, and experienced lifters are usually happy to answer a specific technical question. 'Can you check my set-up?' is a completely normal thing to say.

How to structure the first month

Two or three full-body sessions a week, each with a squat, a hinge, a push, a pull and a core movement. Two to three sets of each, 8–12 reps, and stop two reps short of failure. Add a small amount of weight only when you can complete all your sets with perfect form and it feels controlled.

Ignore the mirrors for six weeks. Go by your logbook. The people making the fastest progress in any gym are almost never the people lifting the most weight — they are the ones who turn up three times a week and write it down.
